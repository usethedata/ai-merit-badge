    Merit Badge Concepts

    • Artificial Intelligence
      • Broad term
      • Most of what people think about AI is a chatbot that uses a Large Language Model
      • But there is a wide range of things that can be considered AI
    • Narrow AI
      • Many AI systems are layers of Narrow AI systems that hand off to each other
    • Superintelligent AI
      • Depending on what has transpired, you may need to deal with the 2026 Executive Order regarding defining Superintelligence. My definition is the working technical definition.
    • Machine Learning
      • Neural networks are one of many tools used in Machine Learning

    Additional Concepts

    • These are concepts I think are important for the class, but they aren’t part of the formal requirements
    • They will be important for later sections
    • Sycophant
      • Nearly all LLMs are programmed in ways to make the user feel positive: “That’s a great idea.”
      • This is to help drive engagement, but it is extremely dangerous
    • Large Language Model (LLM)
      • This is what most people think about when they hear “AI”
      • But AI is much more than LLMs
    • Token
      • Token has many definitions
      • This is the one that matters in this context
      • AI systems generally charge based on the number of input and output tokens

    Chess discussion points

    Added for the chess round.
    - Official explanation: AI because the program learns from past games (machine learning)
    - Under the Requirement 1 definition, a chess program that doesn’t learn but plays well would also count as AI
    - It simulates human problem solving and decision making
    - Learning is one way to get there, not the only way
    - Deep Blue (1997) relied mainly on massive search and hand-built evaluation rules, and was still called AI
    - Questions to draw out the distinction
    - If the program never learned from past games, would it still be AI?
    - A calculator also follows fixed rules. Why isn’t it AI? (It computes an answer. A chess program weighs options and chooses.)
    - Does the alarm clock, hand dryer, or sprinkler timer simulate anything a human would think about?
    - Who decides where the line is?
    - Ties to the ground rule: there often isn’t a single “right” answer

    2(e) Timeline: milestones in the development of AI

    (e) Create a timeline with five key milestones in the development of artificial intelligence.
    Examples below, based on my own web searches and information linked from the guide.
    - 1943: McCulloch and Pitts publish a mathematical model of an artificial neuron
    - 1950: Alan Turing publishes “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” and proposes the imitation game (the Turing test)
    - 1956: Dartmouth summer workshop; the term “artificial intelligence” comes from its proposal
    - 1958: Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron, an early neural network that learns from examples
    - 1959: Arthur Samuel’s checkers program, and the term “machine learning”
    - 1966: ELIZA, an early chatbot, at MIT
    - 1970s–1980s: expert systems (for example MYCIN and XCON) put rule-based AI to work in medicine and business
    - AI “winters”: funding and interest collapsed in the mid–1970s and again in the late 1980s
    - 1986: backpropagation is popularized, making multi-layer neural networks trainable
    - 1997: IBM’s Deep Blue defeats world chess champion Garry Kasparov
    - 2011: IBM’s Watson wins Jeopardy!, and Apple introduces Siri
    - 2012: AlexNet wins the ImageNet image-recognition contest, launching the deep learning boom
    - 2016: DeepMind’s AlphaGo defeats Go champion Lee Sedol
    - 2017: Google researchers publish “Attention Is All You Need,” introducing the transformer behind today’s LLMs
    - 2022: ChatGPT is released to the public (2022–11–30)
    - 2024: Nobel Prizes recognize AI work: Physics (Hopfield and Hinton, neural networks) and Chemistry (Baker, Hassabis, and Jumper, protein design and structure prediction)
    - Dates are from general knowledge. Spot-check any you plan to state precisely.

    Requirement 4: Ethics in AI (45 min)

    Do the following and share what you learned with your counselor:

    4(a) Key points to discuss

    (a) Research ethical concerns and responsible use in AI, including bias, privacy, and AI decision-making.
    - Bias
    - AI learns from data, and data reflects the past, including its unfairness
    - Facial recognition: a 2018 MIT study (Gender Shades) found much higher error rates for darker-skinned women
    - Hiring: Amazon scrapped an experimental resume screener in 2018 after it favored men’s resumes
    - Ties to the Employment Screening and Law Enforcement rounds
    - Privacy
    - What you type into a chatbot may be stored and may be used to train future models
    - Photos, voice, location, and browsing history are all data
    - Who owns it, who can see it, and can you take it back?
    - Special care for minors and for other people’s information
    - AI decision-making
    - AI is already used for decisions about people: admissions, hiring, loans, health care, policing
    - Who is responsible when it’s wrong?
    - Can anyone explain the decision, and can it be appealed?
    - Human in the loop vs. human rubber stamp
    - Also worth a mention
    - Made-up answers (hallucinations) stated confidently
    - Sycophancy: telling you what you want to hear
    - Electricity and water use by data centers
    - Jobs and who benefits
    - Creators’ work used for training without permission
    - Academic honesty
    - Responsible use starts with questions
    - Who made it? What data was it trained on?
    - What are the stakes if it’s wrong?
    - Who checks it?

    Round 5: Student Essay Assignment

    Custom scenario
    - Situation: A student is assigned to “Write a 15 page paper on Victor Hugo’s novel Les Misérables.” The student is thinking about using AI to help.
    - The assignment:
    - Read the book:
    - The unabridged novel is over 1,200 pages, and translations and abridged editions differ
    - The movies and the musical are not the book
    - Choose a focus:
    - One theme or question, such as justice vs. mercy, redemption, poverty and social injustice, or law vs. conscience
    - Or one character, such as Valjean or Javert
    - Learn the context:
    - France from 1815 to 1832, including the June Rebellion of 1832
    - Hugo’s own politics and exile, and when the novel was published (1862)
    - Plan:
    - Write a thesis and an outline
    - 15 pages is roughly 4,000 to 4,500 words, double-spaced
    - Support the argument:
    - Quotations and page citations from the text
    - Optionally, secondary sources
    - Finish:
    - Draft, revise, and proofread
    - Cite sources in the format the teacher requires (e.g., MLA)
    - Key considerations:
    - Learning:
    - What is the assignment for? (reading closely, forming and defending an argument, writing clearly)
    - AI can help learning: explaining context and vocabulary, quizzing you on what you read, asking questions about your thesis, giving feedback on a draft you wrote
    - AI can short-circuit learning: writing the paper, summarizing the book in place of reading it, choosing your thesis for you
    - Rules:
    - What are the teacher’s and school’s rules on AI for this assignment? (syllabus, honor code)
    - Is AI use allowed, limited, or prohibited? Does it have to be disclosed?
    - Whose work is it? Most school policies treat AI-written text submitted as your own as plagiarism
    - If the rules aren’t clear, ask the teacher before starting
    - Has the student read the book?
    - An AI summary is not the book, and it can be wrong
    - AI can invent quotations, page numbers, and plot details, so every quotation must be checked against the actual text
    - Reading the book is where the learning and the evidence for the essay come from
    - Non-native speakers:
    - Reading a 1,200-page nineteenth-century novel in a second language is much harder, and Hugo wrote in French
    - Translation, vocabulary, and grammar tools can legitimately help
    - Where is the line between help with language (grammar, phrasing, translating your own ideas) and AI generating the ideas and the text?
    - AI-detection tools are unreliable, and research has found they are more likely to flag non-native writers’ work as AI-written (Liang et al., 2023)
    - Being open with the teacher about how you used AI is protective
    - Developing ideas in a first language and then writing them in English yourself can be a strength
    - Other students:
    - What if a classmate has AI write the whole paper?
    - Scout Law connections:
    - Trustworthy: Being honest about whose work it is and how AI was used
    - Obedient: Following the school’s and teacher’s rules on AI
    - Brave: Asking the teacher before starting, and doing the hard work of reading the whole book
    - Kind: Considering classmates who face extra challenges, such as reading in a second language
    - Discussion questions:
    - Where is the line between getting help and having AI do the work?
    - What should the student do before using AI at all?
    - Does it matter whether the student has read the book? What is lost if not?
    - How could the student use AI to learn more, not less?
    - How should a student’s language background change what help is appropriate?
    - What if a classmate uses AI to write the whole paper and gets a better grade?

    4(d) The Turing test

    (d) What is the Turing test?
    - Proposed by Alan Turing in 1950 as the “imitation game”
    - A human judge has a text conversation with two hidden parties, one human and one machine
    - If the judge can’t reliably tell which is the machine, the machine passes
    - It tests behavior, not understanding
    - Searle’s Chinese Room (1980) argues that convincing conversation isn’t the same as understanding
    - Recent results
    - A 2025 study (Jones and Bergen) reported that GPT–4.5, prompted to act like a person, was judged to be the human 73% of the time in five-minute text chats
    - Passing a conversation test doesn’t mean the system is general or superintelligent
    - Good follow-up: does passing the test mean it’s intelligent?

    Requirement 5: Deepfakes (25 min)

    Do the following and share what you learned with your counselor:
    Keep it age-appropriate for ages 12 to 17. Stay at the pattern level. No explicit descriptions.

    5(a) What a deepfake is and how it can affect an individual

    (a) Explain what a deepfake is and how it can affect an individual.
    - Definition
    - Images, video, or audio created or altered with AI to realistically show someone saying or doing something they never said or did
    - Includes voice clones
    - Ways it can affect an individual
    - Embarrassment, bullying, and harassment
    - Damage to reputation, friendships, and opportunities
    - Scams: a cloned voice calls a parent or grandparent asking for money
    - Fake sexual images, a serious form of abuse
    - Distress, even when everyone knows it’s fake
    - Doubt: real evidence can be dismissed as “just a deepfake”
    - Spotting them
    - Visual tells (odd hands, glitches, mismatched audio) are unreliable and keep getting less reliable
    - Better habits: check the source, look for the original, and verify with the person through a different channel
    - A family code word helps with voice-clone scams
    - Current developments are about proving that something is authentic. If it can’t be proven to be authentic, then assume it is a fake. Similar to what is done in selling antiques. – C2PA: Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity aka Content Credentials.

    5(b) What to do if you or someone you know is affected

    (b) Describe what actions to take if you or someone you know is impacted by a deepfake.
    - Stop: don’t reply to the person, and don’t forward or repost the fake
    - Tell a trusted adult first: parent, troop leader, teacher, or school counselor
    - Keep a record of where it appears: links, usernames, and dates
    - Sexual or intimate images of anyone under 18: don’t screenshot, save, or send the image
    - Write down the link and where it appears instead
    - Report it to the platform
    - Most platforms have a way to report fake or harassing content
    - Federal law now requires covered platforms to have a removal process (see below)
    - Report it to the school if classmates are involved
    - Law enforcement
    - Report it to local police, especially for threats, extortion, or sexual images
    - For child exploitation, NCMEC’s CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org) takes reports (from general knowledge, not rechecked)
    - Support the person
    - Don’t blame them
    - Don’t pass the image around, even to “show how bad it is”
    - If a scout tells me this has happened to them
    - Don’t probe in front of the group
    - Talk privately, following Youth Protection, and bring in a parent or troop leader

    Take It Down Act (verified 2026–10–01)

    • Signed 2025–05–19
    • Makes it a federal crime to knowingly publish nonconsensual intimate images
      • Covers AI-generated “digital forgeries” of identifiable people
      • Penalties of up to 2 years for adult victims and up to 3 years for minor victims (per Orrick’s summary)
    • Covered platforms (sites, apps, and services built on user-generated content) must have a removal process
      • Must remove the reported content and known identical copies within 48 hours of a valid request
      • Took effect 2026–05–19, and the FTC enforces it

    NCMEC Take It Down service (verified 2026–10–01)

    • takeitdown.ncmec.org, free, operated by the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children
    • For nude, partially nude, or sexually explicit images or videos taken when the person was under 18
      • If the image was taken at 18 or older, NCMEC points to StopNCII.org
    • Works by making a digital fingerprint (hash) on your own device
      • The image isn’t uploaded
      • Participating platforms scan for matches and remove them
    • Their warning: do NOT send, share, or download any image or video to submit to Take It Down
    • Their page doesn’t say whether AI-generated images are covered
      • Check before telling the class that it handles deepfakes

    Requirement 6: Developing AI Skills (30 min)

    Do the following and share what you learned with your counselor:

    6(a) The learning process for AI and its limitations

    (a) Discuss the learning process for AI and its limitations.
    - How it learns
    - Machine learning: find patterns in huge amounts of example data, with no hand-written rules
    - LLMs: trained to predict the next token in text
    - Then tuned with human feedback to be more helpful, which is also where sycophancy comes from
    - It doesn’t “know” things the way people do
    - Limitations
    - Garbage in, garbage out: bad or biased data gives bad or biased results
    - Hallucination: confident, fluent, wrong
    - Training cutoff: doesn’t know recent events unless it can search
    - No built-in fact check
    - Struggles outside what it was trained on
    - Non-deterministic: ask twice and the answers differ
    - Limited memory: it only works with what’s in the conversation (Scoutly starts fresh with every question)
    - Can’t explain its own reasoning reliably
    - Tie back to Requirement 1: machine learning, LLM, token, non-deterministic, sycophant

    6(b) Five methods to communicate effectively with AI

    (b) Identify five methods of how to effectively communicate with AI.
    - Interfaces:
    - Typed chat: Text in a chatbot such as Claude, ChatGPT, or Scoutly. Clear, specific wording matters most here.
    - Voice: Speaking to an assistant such as Siri, Alexa, or a chatbot’s voice mode. Short, clear requests work best, and you need to check that it heard you correctly.
    - Images and camera: Showing it a photo, screenshot, or live camera view and asking about it, or describing a picture you want it to generate.
    - Built into other apps: AI features inside tools you already use, such as a writing assistant that works on text you highlight, autocomplete, or search summaries. You communicate by selecting content and giving a short instruction.
    - Code and agents: Programs that call AI through an API, or agent tools that take actions for you, such as Cowork or Code. Here you give general directions and set limits on what the AI may do.
    - Recommendation feeds: Your clicks, likes, and watch time act as signals to a recommendation engine.

    6(c) Why prompt engineering matters

    (c) Explain the importance of prompt engineering when using AI to create better output.
    - The same AI gives very different results depending on the prompt
    - A better prompt means less rework, fewer errors, and output that fits your need
    - Example for the class
    - Weak: “Write about Les Misérables.”
    - Better: “I’m a 10th grader. Explain in five bullet points how Javert and Valjean represent law versus mercy. Use plain language. Don’t write my paper.”
    - It’s a skill, not magic
    - A good prompt still needs a checked answer
    - A good prompt can ask for help thinking, not for the finished work
